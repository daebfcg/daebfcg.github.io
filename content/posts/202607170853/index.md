### Controls Complexity

#### Pickup Selection

As it turns out, the pickup selector toggle is really unnecessary, and I could have used 3-position switches for the pickups themselves.  So each pickup could be in one of three states: Off, Tapped, Full. My initial thinking was that a quick and convenient way to switch between neck-only, both, or bridge-only would be really handy and what most players would want to be able to do.  However, in practice since these pickups sound so great together in series, that switching is rarely used.

The individual 3-state toggles for each pickup was actually used on some early Schecter guitars.

So this would have eliminated one control.

Also, sadly, the AxLabs toggle is not rock-solid and seems prone to flakiness.  It could be my installation technique, maybe a wire is interfering with one of the spring leaves?

#### Mid Tone knob

The more I use this, the more I think it could be just a switch instead of a variable resistor.  There is not much range between on or off.  It could be due to using a 1M pot.  Maybe a 500K would be more usable.

### Pore Filling

I had thought about pore-filling the walnut areas prior to finishing, using OBrien's shellac-sawdust technique.  I forgot though.  Not a big deal but would have made the finish gloss more consistent between the walnut and cherry/maple (closed-pore wood) sections.

### Ergonomics

The guitar would be more comfortable to play if it had strat-style arm and belly bevels.  I was thinking I might try this but decided not to due to the thinner body (only 1.5 inches thick compared to 1.75 thick Fenders).  Also the maple top and back plates could have looked weird if the bevels were deeper than their thickness.

### Cost

The parts alone for this guitar came in around $500.  The good thing is that they can be collected slowly over time if a one-time purchase of everything is too expensive.  But this is going to deter making any more of these guitars for the mere fun of it.

### Fret markers

I am still not sure what to think of these reconstituted-stone turquoise dots.  I was initially hoping they would be more greenish, as turquoise often is, but these are very blue.  This troubled me at first.  Turquoise on walnut should in theory look great, but there is something slightly off and disconcerting about the color contrast.

They really jump out and create a visual dissonance.  Sometimes I think this is a good thing, as it really stirs up some odd feelings which can make playing this guitar a more intense experience.  Maybe it is just the intensity of the blue.

I guess the look is growing on me.

## Takeaways

### Invest in templates

The tedium and inaccuracy of locating the exact drilling points for the tuners, bridges, and ferrules highlights the need for some pre-made templates.  These need to be designed on the computer and fabricated by CNC or 3D printing.

### Electronics

- Multiple single-coil pickups in series should be explored further

- The option of a capacitor to ground from the pickup series junction is great.  A switch might be all that is needed instead of a pot, so consider that for a more simple operation.

- Tokyo Cosmos pots are great, if a bit stiff.  They do need a casing-grounding solution, though.

- Carling heavy-duty switches work well but are big

### Tung Oil For Fretboards

Non-toxic and highly durable. First coat should be mixed with D-Limonene for penetration.  Keep in mind it takes a week to dry.

### Stainless Steel Frets

Should be the go-to frets for an artisan guitar.  Get the SS frets pre-radiused from Philadelphia Luthier Tools.  Also good are the EVO/Gold (from StewMac).  Use either of these from now on - no more nickel-silver except for quick re-frets.
